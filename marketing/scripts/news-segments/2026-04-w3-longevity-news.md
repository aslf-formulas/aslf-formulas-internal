[STORY 1 — 0:06–0:40]
**A new combination drug extended mouse lifespan 10 percent — and 33 percent of remaining life**

"Number 1. A novel combination drug called SRN-901 just posted some of the strongest lifespan data we've seen in a single paper. Seragon Biosciences, published this April in Drug Design, Development and Therapy. They took 18-month-old mice — already aging — and gave them a single oral combo that hits five pathways at once: an NAD+ precursor, a mitophagy activator, a senolytic, an antioxidant, and an mTOR modulator. Result: median lifespan up 10.7 percent. Remaining lifespan up 33 percent. Frailty progression cut by 70 percent. Remember the NAD+ consensus and the senolytic Phase 1 we covered the last two weeks — this is the direction the field is heading. Not one molecule. Five, working together."

---

[STORY 2 — 0:40–1:15]
**An FDA-approved antiretroviral just lowered biological age in healthy adults**

"Number 2. A twelve-week human trial just showed something striking. Descovy — an FDA-approved antiretroviral originally developed for HIV — reduced measurable markers of biological aging in healthy adults. Across multiple aging clocks, the participants came out looking biologically younger than when they started. Inflammation dropped along with it. What makes this wild: a closely related version of the same drug showed nothing. Same class, different formulation, completely different result. The leading theory is that these drugs quiet down rogue pieces of old DNA in our cells that get noisier with age. If that holds up, an entire class of drugs already sitting in pharmacies becomes a longevity tool."

---

[STORY 3 — 1:15–1:50]
**Gene therapy just reversed aging markers in monkeys**

"Number 3. Researchers just moved a cellular aging therapy into non-human primates — and it worked. A gene therapy using the Box A domain of a protein called HMGB1 restored key aging markers in aged monkeys, including apolipoprotein E — a protein tied to brain aging and Alzheimer's risk. Levels shifted closer to what you'd see in younger animals. Mice are one thing. Primates are the last step before humans. This moves the field meaningfully closer to a clinic."

---

[STORY 4 — 1:50–2:25]
**Lifelong learning linked to 38 percent lower Alzheimer's risk**

"Number 4. A study out of Rush University Medical Center, published in Neurology, just put a number on something researchers have suspected for years. Adults in the top ten percent of lifetime cognitive enrichment — reading, writing, learning new languages, taking on mentally demanding work — had a 38 percent lower risk of developing Alzheimer's compared to those in the bottom ten percent. Onset delayed by about five years. It's not a cure. 21 percent of the high-enrichment group still developed Alzheimer's. But this is one of the largest protective effects we have for dementia — and it costs nothing. Keep learning. Your brain is building reserve."

---

[STORY 5 — 2:25–2:55]
**The world's top longevity researchers just agreed: aging isn't what we thought**

"Number 5. Remember a couple weeks back we mentioned the shift happening in Berlin — the idea that aging isn't a single defect, it's your systems losing the ability to coordinate. This week the World Congress on Targeting Longevity concluded, and the consensus is locking in. Metabolism, immunity, mitochondria, the microbiome — aging is a communication breakdown between them. Why does this matter? Because it changes what a longevity drug needs to do. Single-target drugs are giving way to multi-system interventions — which is exactly why Story 1 tonight hit five pathways at once. The field is reorganizing in real time."
