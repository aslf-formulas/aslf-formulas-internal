Three things that happened in longevity this week, brought to you by ASLF Formulas. Let's get into it.

**Story 1 — BioAge Labs [BLOCKED — see Open Blockers #1]**
BioAge Labs just went public this week, pushing total funding to about $368 million, including the IPO. We covered them last week, dosing the first patient in a Phase 2 trial of BG-102, an oral drug targeting a key inflammation pathway. But the real story is the method. Instead of starting with the disease, BioAge studied people who age unusually slowly and built their drug from that biology — longevity-first, disease second, which is still rare in pharma. If BG-102 reads out well, it validates the whole approach, and every longevity biotech gets benchmarked against it.

**Story 2 — Unregulated peptide market**
Number two, a new peer-reviewed paper reviewed the unregulated peptide market. Injectable compounds sold online as research use only but used openly as personal supplements in biohacking circles. Researchers tie it to GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide, which normalized injectable metabolic therapy and pulled that comfort into a market with far less oversight. These compounds range from well-studied to untested — slick branding, certificates of analysis, scientific names, influencer protocols. It makes it impossible to tell which is which. The authors favor education over regulation, but the message is clear. Unverified compounds packaged as science hurt the credibility of the whole field, including the research that we cover here.

**Story 3 — Brain aging / microglia [hedge placement — see Open Blockers #3]**
And number three, a study that could reshape how we think about brain aging. Scientists profiled the human brain at the single-cell level across the adult lifespan. And they found that microglia, which are the brain's resident immune cells, long thought stable since early development, they appear to progressively be replaced by a different, more inflammatory cell type from the blood. The catch? This was invisible to standard gene-expression tests. It only showed up in DNA methylation data, which is why it's been missed. Until now. Several brain-inflammation therapies assume that it's the same old cells just misbehaving. If this holds up, it's a different cell entirely, which changes the treatment target. Researchers want confirmation, but it's already got labs rethinking what brain aging actually is.
