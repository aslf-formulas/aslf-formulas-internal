[STORY 1 — 0:06–0:44]
**The first patient just received a "cell-rewinding" therapy**

"Number 1. For the first time, a therapy designed to rewind cells to a younger state has been given to a human patient. Life Biosciences — co-founded by Harvard's David Sinclair — just dosed the first person in an early safety trial of a 'partial reprogramming' therapy. The idea is to gently reset aged cells toward a more youthful state — in this case, the nerve cells behind a form of vision loss. For years, cellular reprogramming lived almost entirely in mice and lab dishes. Now it's in a human — and if it proves safe, it's the first real step toward testing whether aging in our tissues can be reset, not just slowed down."

---

[STORY 2 — 0:44–1:14]
**Scientists built the first human map of "zombie" cells**

"Number 2. Scientists just built the first detailed human map of 'zombie' cells. These are senescent cells — cells that stop dividing but refuse to die, and instead leak inflammation that drives aging. A large research consortium mapped where they accumulate across seventeen different human tissues, cell by cell. Until now, most of what we knew came from animals. Here's why it matters: you can't precisely clear these cells until you know exactly where they are and what they look like — so this atlas is the groundwork for the next generation of senolytics, therapies that flush out aging cells without harming healthy ones."

---

[STORY 3 — 1:14–1:46]
**Your grip strength may be partly in your brain**

"Number 3. Your grip strength might be partly in your brain. We usually think of a weak grip as a muscle problem — but researchers found that a map of the brain's wiring, taken from an MRI scan, could predict handgrip strength in older adults. In other words, some of what we call physical frailty may actually begin in the brain, not just the muscles. Why it matters: grip strength is already one of the simplest predictors of how well someone is aging — and this suggests staying strong may be as much about keeping the brain engaged as the body. The practical read: training your body and your mind may not be two separate things."
